title: "The Empty Neural Network Paradox of Modern Recruitment"
description: "Modern recruitment evaluates candidates like a prediction system, but offers no useful gradient for learning from rejection."
pubDate: 2026-08-28

Over the last two years, I’ve been working with different aspects of the AI world, from the basics of prompting to Deep Learning, and everything in between: Agentic AI applications, AI workflows, neural networks, machine learning with Scikit-Learn, LLM fine-tuning and evaluation and, well… you know the story.

The more you learn about AI, the more things you discover that you apparently need to learn. Today, I was approached by a recruiter who didn’t even say hello. I simply received a message followed by a long list of requirements for an LLM Engineer role. And I mean a long list.

Machine Learning, Deep Learning, NLP, Generative AI, PyTorch, TensorFlow, Scikit-Learn, XGBoost, Foundation Models, fine-tuning, MLOps, Multimodal AI, Agentic AI, Computer Vision, Recommendation Systems, Forecasting, Predictive Analytics. There was even an item for “Publishing Machine Learning research.”

That one caught my attention, because now we’re talking about someone who hasn’t simply learned how to use these technologies. We’re potentially talking about someone who has done enough solid work in one of these areas to actually contribute to the development of the field.

But the salary…

Wait for it.

~$4,000 a month.

And perhaps the worst part? I considered it for a few minutes.

Why?

Because looking for a job when you live in LATAM means fewer opportunities. And if you’re in LATAM but you’re not in Mexico, well… things get even more difficult.

That’s when the whole situation stopped being funny for a moment. The current job market punishes you for not knowing something, not having done something, or not being something. You don’t know Kubernetes? Problem. You haven’t worked with a particular framework? Problem. You haven’t deployed something at sufficient scale? Problem. You don’t have enough years of experience with a technology that has existed for approximately twelve minutes? Apparently also a problem.

Fair enough. There will always be things we don’t know. That’s part of working in technology, and especially AI. Learning is part of the job.

The problem comes after the interview.

Because when the process is over, you’re almost never told what the problem actually was. You simply receive the output: “We’ve decided to move forward with another candidate.”

And that’s it.

For more than 10 years, I believed interview processes were getting better. At least, whenever I was involved in them, I tried to make them better. Starting with the absolute basics: never ghosting a candidate, providing feedback, sometimes providing feedback immediately after an interview if the candidate requested it. Not because every candidate needed a detailed technical report explaining why they weren’t selected, but because if someone spends hours preparing, interviewing, answering questions and going through your process, giving that person some useful information seems like a fairly basic professional courtesy.

But today’s job market increasingly feels like a prediction system.

Except it’s a very strange one.

You go through the forward pass. Your experience, knowledge, personality, technical skills, interview performance and everything else become the inputs. The system produces an output.

Rejected.

So you know there is loss. Something between the expected output and the actual output didn’t match.

But the gradient is always 0.

There is nothing useful to backpropagate.

You don’t know which parameters should change. Was it technical knowledge? Experience? Salary expectations? Communication? A missing technology? Seniority? Location? Another candidate? A keyword? Some arbitrary internal requirement nobody mentioned?

Who knows.

Feedback is forbidden knowledge.

And without that information, the candidate is expected to somehow optimize for the next iteration.

I call this The Empty Neural Network Paradox of Modern Recruitment.

The system evaluates you. The system generates an output. The output tells you that something was wrong. But the system provides almost no information that allows you to learn from that output. And then you’re expected to improve before the next forward pass.

Machine Learning would be considerably less impressive if we trained models this way.

Run another epoch.

And going back to that ~$4,000/month LLM Engineer role, the requirements make the whole thing even more interesting. They wanted strong knowledge of Machine Learning, Deep Learning, Natural Language Processing and Generative AI, along with experience using frameworks such as PyTorch, TensorFlow, Scikit-Learn and XGBoost. They wanted hands-on experience training, fine-tuning and evaluating models, including Foundation Models.

They also wanted MLOps experience with platforms such as MLflow, AWS SageMaker, Databricks, Kubeflow and Weights & Biases, together with knowledge of model evaluation, hyperparameter tuning, feature engineering and model monitoring. Then came Multimodal AI involving text, images, audio and structured data.

Agentic AI was there too, naturally. The candidate should have experience developing and deploying Agentic AI solutions using frameworks such as LangGraph, AutoGen, CrewAI or OpenAI Agents. The role also mentioned experience with Computer Vision, Recommendation Systems, Forecasting or Predictive Analytics.

Then there was the academic side. An advanced degree in Computer Science, Machine Learning, Artificial Intelligence, Statistics or a related field.

And finally, experience contributing to open-source AI projects, building AI products at scale or publishing Machine Learning research.

For approximately $48,000 a year.

And somewhere in LATAM, someone qualified enough to look at this description and understand every item in it will probably still stop for a few minutes and think:

“Maybe I should apply.”

And perhaps that’s the part of the system that needs the most training.
